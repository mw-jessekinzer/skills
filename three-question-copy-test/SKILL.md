# The Three-Question Copy Test

## Overview
A pass/fail gate for any line of copy. Great copy is specific, verifiable, and ownable. Score every sentence on three questions: three yeses means keep it, anything less means rewrite. This is Midwestern's copy-quality gate.

## When to use
- Writing or reviewing a headline, subhead, tagline, CTA, ad, subject line, or social hook.
- A line feels generic, abstract, salesy, or like something a competitor could say word for word.
- Choosing between copy options.

Not for: long-form body prose where flow matters more than line-level punch. Use it there as a spot-check, not a gate.

## The three questions
Apply to every sentence:

1. **Visualize it?** Can the reader picture a concrete scene, person, or object? Abstract nouns ("talent," "solutions") and adjectives ("remarkable," "world-class") fail. A specific moment ("a senior engineer reviewing your pull requests") passes.
2. **Falsify it?** Could the claim be proven true or false? Puffery ("ready," "best-in-class") can't be tested, so it fails. A checkable claim ("in under 10 business days") passes, and it must be true.
3. **Can nobody else say it?** Could a competitor paste this line onto their site and have it be just as true? If yes, it's a category claim, not yours. Ownable copy rests on something only Midwestern can truthfully say.

**Three yeses = ship. Any less = rewrite the failing question.**

## How to apply
- **As a gate (reviewing):** score each line 0 to 3. Rewrite anything under 3. A line that "sort of" visualizes is a no.
- **As a lens (drafting):** draft, test, then rewrite for the one failing question. Usually only one is weak.
- **Diagnose, then fix that question:**

| If it fails... | The fix | The mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Visualize | Replace the abstract noun/adjective with a concrete person + moment (a ritual, artifact, or place). | **Zoom-in ladder.** Write the abstract word at the top of the page, rewrite it downward asking "what do I actually mean here," stop when you hit a physical object. `regain fitness` → `off the couch` → `running 5K` → **Couch to 5K**. |
| Falsify | Swap the vague claim for a real number or fact. Pull it from `[[Proof Points]]`. | **You can't talk, you can only point.** Not "he's funny" but "reads on the tube." Not "gold is a good investment" but the fifty-year chart. Pointing takes research, which is why it works. |
| Ownable | Anchor to something only Midwestern can truthfully say (the true number, the embedded model). | **Never write an ad a competitor can sign.** Ask what we can do that nobody else is doing that buyers care about, then name the thing only we can name. |

**Fourth gate, cheap to run: one Mississippi, two Mississippi.** Look at the line and count. If you don't get it in two seconds, it's out, no matter how well it scores on the other three. Not every reader has to get it: confusion someone forwards beats a bland line nobody mentions. What fails is the line that's already been said ("one drink to replace them all").

## Rules of thumb
- **Adjectives fail by nature.** Replace with a fact, or use one that's verifiable ("senior" is a level; "remarkable" is an opinion).
- **A concrete moment beats an abstract noun.** Name the ritual, artifact, or moment (standup, pull request, critique, roadmap, ship date).
- **The true number is the ownability lock.** Specific beats rounded. Competitors can't copy a number that isn't true for them.
- **Attribute proof to the firm, not the individual** ("from a team that's shipped for 120+ companies," not "talent that's shipped for 120+ teams").
- **Match the concrete detail to the audience.** Discipline-specific artifacts (a PR, a Figma file) picture sharply but narrow the audience; neutral anchors (a roadmap, a sprint) stay broad.

## Common mistakes
- Scoring generously. Be strict; "kind of" is a no.
- Chasing a wow adjective. The framework punishes adjectives. Use facts.
- Passing on falsify alone. A true-but-generic line still fails ownable.
- Inventing numbers to pass falsify. Use only validated stats from `[[Proof Points]]`.

## Going deeper: the craft mechanics
The gate above judges a line. `references/craft-principles.md` (in this skill) is how you build one. **Read it whenever the gate says rewrite and the fix isn't obvious**, or when the task is bigger than a single line (an ad concept, a positioning line, a newsletter open, a full page).

What's in there, so you know when to open it:

- **Concreteness** — the zoom-in ladder, worked twice. Abstract is anything you can't drop on your foot.
- **Facts as raw material** — distinct from falsify. A fact is where a line comes from, not the line itself. "There are 350 million Americans" is true and inert. Includes expanding the time frame to make a small number land (4 hrs/day → 22,000 hours).
- **Enemy and conflict** — three types (approach, belief, competitor), and Luke Sullivan's line-down-the-page. Never open with "our positioning is good," open with how everyone else does it.
- **Ownability** — the Volvo speedometer ad, the Kitson lesson, and why the wrapper is the product (Snickers vs Fuse).
- **Speed of comprehension** — the two-second gate and Neil French's subtraction rule (headline, subhead, picture, logo: use one and win).
- **The three pieces, in order** — who you're talking to, having something to say, saying it well. Most people start at the third.
- **Rewriting** — Kaplan's Law of Words, the burrito test for paragraphs, and why you show three versions instead of one when you want useful feedback.
- **Structure** — dividing lines and parallelism, reframing cost as saving, keeping the claim small enough to believe.
- **The 25-rewrite walkthrough** — one ad, step by step, including the step where fixing one problem created another.
- **Example bank** — 16 lines with what each one is doing mechanically.

Two working rules from that doc worth applying by default: **write in the medium** (draft the page in the page, the ad in Figma, never in a Word doc, because line breaks change the work), and **done means you can't remove a word**.
