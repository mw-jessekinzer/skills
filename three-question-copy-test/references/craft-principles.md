
# Copywriting Craft Principles (Harry Dry)

**What this is.** The mechanics behind the three-question gate in `SKILL.md` (canonical vault copy: `The Three-Question Copy Test` in the Marketing Hub). That gate judges a line: visualize / falsify / own. This is *how you actually get a line to pass*, plus everything else in the source the gate doesn't cover. Gate to judge, this to fix.

**Source.** Harry Dry (Marketing Examples) interviewed on *How I Write* with David Perell. Transcript processed 2026-08-11. Examples are kept deliberately: Harry's whole argument is that examples beat theory, so stripping them would contradict the source. Named attributions are kept as retrieval hooks.

## Quick reference

| Move | Use it when |
|---|---|
| Zoom-in ladder | A line is abstract and you don't know what you actually mean |
| Point, don't talk | You've written adjectives instead of evidence |
| Start with a fact | You have nothing to say, or you're saying wallpaper |
| Expand the time frame | A true number is too small to land |
| Line down the page, two opposites | You need conflict and don't have any |
| One Mississippi, two Mississippi | Judging whether a line lands |
| Kaplan's Law of Words | Every rewrite, always |
| Burrito test | Checking whether a paragraph holds together |
| Write it in the medium | Always, before the first draft |
| Three versions side by side | Asking someone for feedback |

## 1. Concreteness

**Rule:** if the reader can't see it, they won't remember it. Lisa Cron: "If I can't see it, it's not there yet."

**The test Harry runs on people:** six words, read aloud, then asked which they remember. Concrete: charging pit bull, muscly Irishman, leg of lamb. Abstract: seamless transition, better way. Everyone recalls the concrete ones and forgets the rest.

**The definition worth keeping:** abstract is anything you can't drop on your foot. Drop "a better way" on your foot and it fades away. Drop a muscly Irishman on your foot and it hurts.

**The mechanism: the zoom-in ladder.** Draw a line down the left of a page. Write "abstract" at the top, "concrete" at the bottom. Put the offending word at the top, then rewrite it down the page, asking each time: what do I actually mean here? Stop when you hit a physical object.

Worked example:

- regain fitness (abstract)
- getting people off the couch after six months of sitting
- running
- running 5K
- **Couch to 5K** (concrete)

That is the name of the most downloaded fitness program ever, built by a Boston TV producer, not Nike.

Second worked example, the New Balance line. The weak version: "worn by pretty people in big cities and old people in non-big cities." The zoomed-in version: **"Worn by supermodels in London. And dads in Ohio."** Supermodel is a thing everyone can see. Ohio dad is a thing everyone can see. The juxtaposition does the rest.

**Related: metonymy.** Substituting a visual shorthand for the literal term. Apple's "1,000 songs in your pocket" is literally wrong (the songs are in a media player), and that is why it works. Pocket is more visual, more surprising, more punchy. Contrast the PC ads of the same era, which sold gigabytes.

## 2. Falsifiability

**Rule:** write sentences that could be proven true or false. A falsifiable sentence puts your head on the chopping block, and readers sit up for it.

**Why it works:** Galileo got house arrest for "the earth goes round the sun." If he had written "the earth has a harmonious connection with a celestial object," nobody would have cared enough to punish him.

**The mechanism: you can't talk, you can only point.** (Found in a Reddit thread by a now-deleted account.) Set a friend up on a blind date. Round one, no objective claims allowed: he's funny, he's intelligent, good values, great family. That is talking. Round two, only claims that can be checked: six foot two, looks like Ryan Gosling, reads on the tube. That is pointing.

It gets you off the adjective trail. Instead of "gold is a great investment," point at the fifty-year chart, point at what gold did in the last crash, point at how much of it is in Warren Buffett's portfolio. Pointing takes research, which is exactly why it's better.

**For Midwestern:** pointing is what [[Proof Points]] is for. The validated delivery-speed numbers (full-time recruited in under 15 business days, embedded placed in under 10) are the point-at-the-chart version of "we're fast."

## 3. Facts as raw material

Distinct from falsifiability. Falsifiability is a property of a sentence. A fact is where the sentence comes from.

**Rule:** if in doubt, give me a fact. Then make something out of it.

**Why:** most people open their mouths and say nothing. Harry's phrase for it: "wallpaper, word-shaped air, balloon smoke." A fact guarantees you said something. It is true, it came from somewhere, and there is a story behind it.

**A fact alone is not interesting.** "There are 350 million Americans" is a fact and it's inert. The fact is the seed, not the ad. You have to work it.

**Facts don't have to be numbers.** Fast food places refill Heinz bottles with generic factory ketchup. That's a fact Harry saw on the news. The ad it becomes: a woman pouring the non-Heinz into the Heinz bottle, handing it over. **"Even when it's not Heinz, it's Heinz."** The line is not the fact. It came from one.

**Precision is credibility.** The bad sports pundit says Tiger "didn't want it enough today" or "looked sloppy on the range." The good one says he normally hits 11 fairways a round and hit seven, made up for it with 30% more greens in regulation, and was three times more likely to one-putt. Same round, and only one version can be argued with.

**Don't name the abstraction, show the reality.** Don't say "inequality." Show the graph.

**Technique: expand the time frame to make a small number big.** A marketer writes maybe four hours a day. Four hours a day is unremarkable. Four hours a day, 300 days a year, over a 30-year career is **22,000 hours**. Same fact, different frame.

## 4. Enemy and conflict

**Rule:** put something on the other side of the line. We judge everything relatively.

**The image for it:** pickle juice next to orange juice. The pickle juice makes the orange sweeter, the orange makes the pickle sourer. Neither works as hard alone.

**Three types of enemy (A, B, C):**

- **A: approach.** A different way of solving the same problem.
- **B: belief.** I think this, you think that.
- **C: competitor.** Apple vs PC.

**The mechanism (Luke Sullivan):** draw a line down the middle of the page and write any two conflicts that come to mind. Do not overthink it. Red and blue. Before and after. Problem and solution. Their way and your way.

**In practice, Loom's positioning.** Harry never opens with "Loom's positioning is good." He opens with how every other screen recorder positions itself (easier-to-use screen recorder), then Loom: remote communication sucks, Zoom calls are too long, email is too impersonal, so here is async video messaging. Loom exited the screen-recorder bucket and is now worth more than the whole bucket combined.

**The `but` rule for storytelling.** You are trying to hinge the story on the word "but" without writing "but." Not "I got the train, I had a coffee, we started talking," which is a list. Instead: I woke up at 8:52 and started picking clothes, **but** my mum ran up the stairs and told me I couldn't do the interview in those shorts.

## 5. Ownability

**Rule (Durkee, as named in the interview):** never write an ad a competitor can sign.

**Why it matters beyond differentiation:** it forces you to look harder at what you're actually selling. You cannot answer "what can only we say" without learning something.

**The example: the Volvo speedometer ad.** "Your car has five numbers on the speedometer. Volvo has six. One could get the impression that the people who made your car lack a little confidence." Nobody else can write that. The only route to the line is getting in the car and counting.

**Hinge: "the dating app designed to be deleted."** The brief was probably "an app for people who want long-term relationships and are fed up of dating apps," which is not memorable. The writer's job turned it into alliteration (designed, deleted) plus juxtaposition (who builds a product that wants to be deleted). Hinge now owns the phrase. Harry's summary: **write copy that can't be copied.**

**The Kitson lesson: what can I do that nobody else is doing that people care about?** Dave Kitson was a mediocre Premier League striker, one England cap. He outsold the memoirs of Beckham, Lampard, Gerrard and Owen combined. He couldn't offer a glittering career, so he offered the thing the stars would never do: an undercover account of the Premier League, gambling addictions, what happens when England go abroad. And because nobody wants a book by Dave Kitson, he signed it **The Secret Footballer**. Objectively the worse product, outsold them on positioning and storytelling alone.

**The wrapper is the product.** Snickers ("you're not you when you're hungry") is the best-selling chocolate bar in the world. Fuse ("only to be eaten wearing rubber-soled shoes") was discontinued in 2006. Same shape, same taste. Strip away wrapper, copy and branding and most businesses do roughly the same thing. Samsung and Apple both have an app store and both take good pictures, and 17 of 20 American teenagers pick Apple. Ogilvy: **we don't choose the whiskey, we choose the image.**

## 6. Speed of comprehension

**The gate: one Mississippi, two Mississippi.** Look at the ad, count. If you don't get it inside two seconds, it's out. Sometimes you know more in two seconds than in two hours.

**Neil French's subtraction rule.** Most people think an ad needs four elements: headline, subhead, picture, logo. If you can make one work with a single element, you're onto a winner.

The Economist ad is the proof: **"I've never read The Economist. Management trainee, aged 42."** No picture, no logo, no real subhead. It doesn't look like an ad, so people read it. Two more things doing quiet work: the background is Economist red, which people already recognize, and the visual hierarchy stages the joke. You read the confession, then the attribution lands, then you have a story.

**Not everyone has to get it.** Athletic Greens: "You're gonna need a smaller cabinet." Perell didn't get it. Harry's read: if everyone gets it, you're probably doing something wrong. Confusion that gets texted to a friend beats a bland line nobody mentions. The failure mode is not "some people miss it," it's "one drink to replace them all," which has been said before.

**First line, second line (Dave Gerhardt, via Joe Sugarman).** The only job of the first line is to get you to the second. Tesla: "It takes 3.1 seconds to read this ad." Wait, what? "The same time it takes a Model S to go 0 to 60." Ad complete. It also breaks the fourth wall and the number is precise, which reads as true.

## 7. The three pieces of the process

Harry's whole definition of copywriting, in order.

### Piece one: who you're talking to

Someone on the tube glancing at a phone is not someone who has used your app for six months without upgrading.

**Failure case: Snapchat's Super Bowl ad.** Seven million on "more Snapchat, less social media." Average Super Bowl viewer is 39. Nobody asked who they were talking to.

**Success case: the St Ives café.** One café put out a blown-up menu with every latte price and seventeen croissants. The other put out **"coffee and pastry = £5."** The queue was at the second one. The reader is a tourist half-looking while walking to the beach. Form follows function. Your job is to tell them they need a coffee, not to grow your Instagram.

**How to keep the reader's conditions in view:** put the work where it will live. Don't judge a billboard as a clean Photoshop file. Drop it into a photo with seven competing ads and people walking past, then ask if it grabs you. (Attributed to Ogilvy.)

### Piece two: you have to have something to say

You need a belief, not just a product. Build for something you think is wrong with the world, and the copy gets much easier.

Harry's own: every marketer should learn to write, it is the number one skill in marketing. Marketing Examples exists because learning marketing meant two pages of theory when he wanted examples. Zuora's investors weren't buying the product, they were buying the belief that everything would become a subscription (Andy Raskin's territory). Dave Gerhardt's question to Harry: why did you start this in the first place?

### Piece three: say it well

This is the only part most people think copywriting is: rhythm, visuals, persuasion. It's the third step, not the first.

## 8. Simplicity and rewriting

**You cannot write simply. You can only rewrite simply.**

**Kaplan's Law of Words:** any word that isn't working for you is working against you. **Harry's Law of Words:** you are not taking Kaplan's Law seriously enough.

**The law applies to ideas, not just words.** The strength of an idea is inversely proportional to its scope. This is why "and" on a landing page is usually a warning: "we sell jeans and t-shirts and socks and we have dressing rooms." Hiut Denim: **"We make jeans. That's it."** Album covers work the same way: the Velvet Underground banana, the Dark Side prism, the Aladdin Sane lightning bolt. Bowie also made *Reality*, seven disparate images, and nobody can describe it.

**The burrito test for paragraphs.** A good paragraph should survive being thrown across the room without coming apart. Sentences shouldn't look awkwardly at each other, they should add up to more than their parts. **The check:** pull one sentence out. If the paragraph still works, that sentence shouldn't have been there.

**Short paragraphs are monkey bars.** Harry has not written a paragraph longer than two lines since March 2021. Three reasons: readers on a train need something easy to swing between; a three-line paragraph usually means he hasn't understood the idea well enough yet; and good lines need room to breathe.

**Keep the versions.** Copy, paste, rewrite, and end up with four or five versions of the same paragraph. Two payoffs. It lets you refine, and more importantly it gives you permission to be terrible, which is what lets you be different. It also transforms feedback: show one paragraph and you get "I like it" or "I don't." Show three and you get "that one's best, but use that sentence from the second one."

**Spitballing when you're stuck (the Ed Sheeran tap).** Write without judging: go full Zuckerberg, scroll down, roll monopoly dice, spaghetti at wall. The tap runs dirty at first. Keep it flowing and it clears. That run ended at "throw money and pray."

## 9. Structure

**Rule:** dividing lines and parallelism. A big scary subject becomes usable the moment you divide it.

Copywriting is big and vague until it's three buckets: who are you talking to, what are you saying, how are you saying it.

**The move (Murray Davis, "That's Interesting," 1971):** take two things people think are different and show they're one; take one thing and show it's two.

Harry's essay applied it: "the first step in getting 10,000 people to read your work is getting 100 people to read your work." That sentence *is* the structure. Part one, things that don't scale, the first 100. Part two, momentum, the next 9,900.

Without structure, a book is just long and heavy. Chapters are the structure. Seventeen course videos need the same thing: give me something to hold on to.

**Parallelism is a real constraint, not decoration.** "Marketers, you'll spend 22,000 hours of your career writing. Spend two learning how to do it well." The real number was around 20,000. Harry wrote 22,000 for the 22 / 2 echo. On the Cybertruck line, Perell's edit was structural: "Better truck than an F-150, faster than a Porsche 911" becomes **"Tougher than an F-150, faster than a 911."** Tougher is more concrete than better, it kills the word "truck," it's shorter, and it makes the two halves parallel.

**Reframe cost as saving.** "Learn copywriting" costs two hours, which is a lot when you're busy. "You'll spend 22,000 hours writing, spend two learning to do it well" turns the same two hours into a saving.

**Make the claim small enough to believe.** The course ad promises 1% to 2% conversion, not "become a millionaire." One percent to two percent is doable, and it is also a 100% increase. Sincerity is the asset.

**Make the customer feel smart.** "The sport sedan for people who inherited brains instead of wealth." The car is unremarkable, so the ad sells the buyer's judgment instead. Everyone wants to be the person with brains rather than money.

**Juxtaposition as the whole idea.** De Beers, 1948, Mary Frances Gerety: **"How can you make two months' salary last forever?"** Two months of salary against forever. You can't, which is why you read the answer: the diamond engagement ring. Before that campaign, engagement rings were sapphires, rubies and emeralds. Now roughly 85% are diamonds.

## 10. Craft conditions

**Write in the medium, never in a Word doc.** Newsletter goes straight into ConvertKit. Ad goes straight into Figma. Billboard gets mocked up among other billboards, in place. A line that spills from one line to three changes the work, and you can't see that in a doc. "I don't like doing stuff not where it is."

**Copy and design are the same job.** The 25-rewrite process below happened in Figma and would have been impossible in a text editor.

**Standards are the work.** "Your work is nothing but your standards." 9 out of 10 or it doesn't go out. **Done feels like this:** you walk around the garden, come back, and can't take anything away. "Worn by supermodels in London. And dads in Ohio." You can't add a word or remove a word and improve it.

**Input discipline.** Silence and action. No TikTok or Instagram first thing, because the day goes to drudge. Writing retreats where nobody speaks until dinner, which creates an intensity. A walk in the park with no earphones.

## 11. The 25-rewrite walkthrough

The most transferable part of the interview: two days, about 25 rewrites, one ad. What actually happened, in order.

1. **Seed.** A tweet seen four years earlier: the difference between 1% and 2% is not 1%, it's 100%. Written down as a possible argument for learning copywriting.
2. **Conflict.** Luke Sullivan's line down the page. On one side, increase landing page conversions from 1% to 2%. On the other, spend twice as much on ads.
3. **Frame.** "Want to grow twice as fast? You've got two choices."
4. **Borrowed layout.** An old Volkswagen ad, "how to prepare your car for winter," with a long checklist for the ordinary car and one item for the VW. Harry loved the layout, dropped in placeholder images and copied the checklist squares.
5. **Fill the left column.** Raise twice the cash, hire twice the staff, spend twice as much on ads, cross both your fingers.
6. **Headings for parallelism.** "The corporate way" vs "the copywriting way."
7. **A problem he refused to live with.** The right-hand option ran to two lines. Fix one: cut "landing page." Fix two: promote "learn to write" to the header, leaving "increase conversions from 1% to 2%."
8. **Solving one problem created another.** With "learn to write" gone from the column, the two headings no longer had parallel structure. One forward, one back. This is the normal condition of the work, not a sign of failure.
9. **Spitballing.** Deliberately bad options until the tap ran clean, landing on **"throw money and pray."**
10. **Feedback on options, not on one draft.** Texted several people whose taste he trusts. Consensus picked "throw money and pray."
11. **A dead end, walked anyway.** Tried a very long Volkswagen-style list. Didn't work. Reverted.
12. **Design last.** Bolded the repetitions already in the copy (twice, both) to pull out the rhythm. Highlighted "twice as fast" in the header. Added the line "that's twice as many," which closes the loop back to the original 1%-to-2% insight.
13. **Art direction as copywriting.** Commissioned illustrations, then directed them: same character in both panels, the praying guy should look like an idiot rather than smiling because we don't like him, and turn the writing guy's pen into a typewriter and add a lightbulb.

The lesson Harry draws: you cannot write the finished ad. You can only build it up piece by piece. And you keep walking down the blind alley to find out whether it's blind.

## 12. What AI does not have

**Taste, conviction, experience.**

**Taste.** Ogilvy pulled "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock" out of a motor magazine. He didn't generate it, he recognized it, and pushed it as the headline. Rolls-Royce sales rose 50%. Generative fill widening the Abbey Road cover into extra trees and a balloon is the opposite: a tool with no taste attached, sold as revolution.

**Conviction.** Ask AI what it believes and it tells you it doesn't believe anything. Writing is: you see something, you believe something, you write about it.

**Experience.** Bukowski worked as a postman for twenty years and wrote *Post Office*. Kerouac lived on the road for seven years and wrote *On the Road* in three weeks. Michael Lewis worked on Wall Street, then waited, then wrote *The Big Short*. Sylvia Plath interned at a New York fashion magazine, and Esther Greenwood is a disillusioned intern at a New York fashion magazine.

Two distinct kinds of experience are in play. Ogilvy's is noticing: go out, be awake, and the line is already in the world waiting to be found. Bukowski's is accumulation: years inside something until you know what nobody outside it knows.

**The failure mode without either.** Asked to write about New York having never been, you produce yellow taxis and the city that never sleeps: "gumming together long strips of words already set in order by somebody else." Which, as Harry notes, is quite literally what a predictive model does.

**John Long, on tools:** pens to typewriters didn't make the work better, typewriters to laptops didn't make the work better, film to digital didn't make the work better. A tool is only as good as your taste.

## 13. Newsletter craft (Marketing Examples)

**Ann Handley:** it's less about the news, more about the letter. So write a letter.

**Open with time and place.** Look out of the window and report what's there. "Live from London. Weather update: trees shuddering, majestic hail." The Chamber of Secrets does the same thing: not for the first time, an argument had broken out over breakfast at number four Privet Drive. Breakfast, an argument, a place. You know where you are.

The one he read out on the podcast:

> Hey it's Harry. It's 3:47am. I'm in the big smoke. Alone. Laptop. Green tea. Looking at the whiteboard like a dozy dog. Six words on it. Five with lines through. Intro glares at me. I glare back. The final horcrux. Sip some tea. Is this any good? I don't know. My tea tastes cold. I should press send.

Nothing clever in it. Six things in every issue, five finished, the intro is the last one left. What he was after: **"out of the oven, fresh bread, warm to touch."** Not polished. Recent.

**Three things he does that most don't:**

1. Five or six examples per issue. Dense.
2. Conflict in nearly every example. Before and after, problem and solution, how everyone does it and how they do it.
3. No paragraph longer than two lines.
